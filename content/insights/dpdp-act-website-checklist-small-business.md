If your website has an enquiry form, a quote request, a newsletter signup or a booking page, you collect personal data, and India's **Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023** (the DPDP Act) applies to you. The **DPDP Rules, 2025** were notified on 13 November 2025, and most of the duties that matter for a business website (a clear notice, valid consent, security, breach reporting and a way for people to reach you about their data) are scheduled to apply from **13 May 2027**. There is no general exemption just because a business is small. The good news: for a typical small business website, getting ready is mostly about a better form, a plain-language notice and a few habits, not an expensive software project.

This guide explains what the law asks of an ordinary business website in plain terms. It is not legal advice; for anything specific to your business, speak to a lawyer or your CA.

## Does the DPDP Act apply to my small business?

The Act applies to **digital personal data**: any information about an identifiable person that is collected online, or collected offline and then digitised. A name and mobile number typed into your contact form is personal data. So is an email address on your newsletter list, a patient's details in a booking form, or a buyer's address in your online store.

If your business decides why and how that data is used, the Act calls you a **Data Fiduciary**. That covers a clinic in Adajan, a textile trader on Ring Road, a CA firm, an exporter and an online store alike. The Act does let the government exempt certain notified classes, such as some startups, from parts of the law, but it has no blanket exemption based on turnover or team size. Assume it applies to you unless your lawyer tells you otherwise.

## The key dates

| Date | What happens |
| --- | --- |
| 13 November 2025 | Rules notified. Provisions setting up the Data Protection Board came into force. |
| 13 November 2026 | Rules for registering Consent Managers came into force. |
| 13 May 2027 | Most duties for businesses apply: notice, consent, security, breach reporting, retention, contact details and grievance handling. |

In early 2026, the Ministry of Electronics and IT (MeitY) floated a proposal to shorten the 18-month window. As of today, the 13 May 2027 date is the one most compliance timelines work to, but check the current position on [MeitY's website](https://www.meity.gov.in/) before you plan around it. Either way, starting now is cheaper than rushing later.

## What changes for your enquiry form

Your enquiry form is where most small businesses collect personal data, so start there.

### 1. Show a clear notice at the point of collection

When you ask for someone's details, the Rules expect a notice that is clear, in plain language, and understandable on its own. It should tell the person:

- **What data you are collecting**, listed item by item (for example: name, mobile number, email, city)
- **Why you are collecting it**, and what you will use it for (for example: to reply to your enquiry and send a quote)
- **How they can withdraw consent**, and that withdrawing should be as easy as giving it
- **How they can exercise their rights** (such as asking for their data to be corrected or erased) and how to complain to the Data Protection Board

On a website, this usually means a short line under the form with a link to a fuller privacy notice. A privacy policy buried in the footer that nobody reads, copied from another website, does not do this job.

### 2. Get real consent, not a pre-ticked box

Consent under the Act must be free, specific, informed, unconditional and unambiguous, given through a clear affirmative action. In practice:

- **No pre-ticked boxes.** The person should tick the box themselves.
- **Separate purposes, separate choices.** Replying to an enquiry and adding someone to a marketing list are different purposes. If you want both, ask for both separately.
- **Ask only for what you need.** If you only need a name and a phone number to call back, don't make date of birth, company size and full address compulsory.

### 3. Be careful with children's data

If your business serves children, such as a coaching institute, a school, a kids' clinic or a toy store, the Act treats anyone under 18 as a child. Processing a child's data needs verifiable consent from a parent or lawful guardian, and tracking, behavioural monitoring and targeted advertising aimed at children are restricted. Forms for these businesses should be designed for the parent to fill in.

## Beyond the form: what else your website needs

### A named contact for data questions

The Rules require you to publish the business contact details of a person who can answer questions about how you handle personal data. For a small business this can simply be the owner or a manager, with an email address, shown in your privacy notice.

### A way to handle requests and complaints

People can ask to see, correct or erase their data, or raise a grievance. The Rules set a maximum of **90 days** to respond to grievances. You don't need software for this. You need one inbox, one responsible person and a simple log of what was asked and when it was answered.

### Reasonable security

The Rules list security measures such as encryption or masking where appropriate, access control, and keeping logs so unauthorised access can be detected, with logs kept for at least one year. For a typical business website that means:

- HTTPS on every page (a padlock in the browser)
- Form submissions going to a business email or system that only the right people can open, not a shared personal Gmail
- Old plugins, themes and admin accounts removed, and strong passwords with two-step login on hosting, domain and email accounts
- A hosting provider that keeps backups and access logs (our [guide to website hosting](/insights/website-hosting-for-small-business-india) covers what to ask for)

### A plan for a data breach

If personal data leaks, for example through a hacked website or a lost laptop, the business must inform affected people and the Data Protection Board without delay, with a detailed report to the Board within **72 hours**. Write down now who would do what, so you are not working it out in a panic.

### Delete what you no longer need

Personal data should be erased once the purpose is served or consent is withdrawn, unless another law (such as tax or accounting rules) requires you to keep it. Leads from three years ago sitting in a spreadsheet with no purpose are a risk, not an asset.

## What about WhatsApp, ads and tracking?

- **WhatsApp enquiries:** a customer who messages you has shared their number with you. Use it to reply to them. Adding them to broadcast lists for promotions is a separate purpose that needs its own consent. Our guide on [WhatsApp Business App vs API](/insights/whatsapp-business-app-vs-api) explains how bulk messaging works.
- **Meta and Google lead forms:** the ad platform collects the details, but once they reach you, you are responsible for how you use them. Add your own privacy notice link to the lead form. We compare these in [Meta Lead Form vs Landing Page](/insights/meta-lead-form-vs-landing-page).
- **Analytics and tracking pixels:** if your site uses tracking tools that collect personal data, mention them in your privacy notice and avoid sending form contents such as names or phone numbers into analytics or ad platforms.

## Penalties, in brief

Penalties are decided by the Data Protection Board and go up to **₹250 crore** for failing to take reasonable security safeguards, up to **₹200 crore** for failing to report a breach or breaking the rules on children's data, and up to **₹50 crore** for other violations, according to the government's [summary of the DPDP Rules](https://static.pib.gov.in/WriteReadData/specificdocs/documents/2025/nov/doc20251117695301.pdf). These are maximums, and the Board is expected to consider the nature and size of the business, but they show why it's worth taking seriously.

## A simple DPDP checklist for your website

- [ ] List every place your website collects personal data: forms, bookings, checkout, newsletter, chat
- [ ] Remove form fields you don't actually need
- [ ] Add a short, plain-language notice under each form, linked to a full privacy notice
- [ ] Replace pre-ticked boxes with an unticked consent box; separate marketing consent from enquiry replies
- [ ] Publish a named contact (and email) for data questions
- [ ] Set up one inbox and a simple log for data requests and grievances
- [ ] Check HTTPS, plugin updates, admin access and two-step login on hosting, domain and email
- [ ] Write a one-page breach plan: who checks, who informs whom, within what time
- [ ] Decide how long you keep leads and customer data, and delete what's past that
- [ ] Add your privacy notice link to Meta and Google lead forms

## FAQ

**Does a one-page website with only a phone number need to comply?** If the site collects no personal data (no form, no signup, no tracking of identifiable people), there is little to do on the website itself. The moment you add an enquiry form, the duties apply. Your offline customer records may still be covered once they are digitised.

**Can I copy a privacy policy from another website?** It's a bad idea. The notice has to describe what *you* collect and why. A copied policy often lists things you don't do and leaves out things you do.

**Is the 13 May 2027 date final?** That is the date the Rules set for most obligations. MeitY has discussed shortening it, so check for updates. Preparing now means a change in date won't catch you out.

**Do I need to hire a Data Protection Officer?** Only businesses notified as Significant Data Fiduciaries must appoint one based in India. A typical small business only needs to publish a contact person who can answer data questions.

**Does this apply to my business if my customers are abroad?** The Act covers personal data processed in India, and also processing outside India when it relates to offering goods or services to people in India. If you serve EU or UK customers, their own data laws may apply too.
